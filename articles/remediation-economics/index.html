How product companies should allocate scarce engineering capacity to the security work that removes the most consequential exposure.

Consider a representative quarterly review.

Security brings more than 100 findings.

Engineering looks at the same quarter and says:

“We can realistically take 20 or 30.”

Not because Engineering does not care about security.

Because the roadmap is already carrying customer commitments, reliability work, on-call demand, production follow-ups, platform modernization, and release dates the business has already promised.

Then somebody opens one of the High-severity tickets.

It looks simple:

Upgrade vulnerable dependency.

The engineer looks closer.

The upgrade changes three downstream APIs.

Several services consume those APIs.

One change requires regression testing across multiple teams.

A “quick security patch” has quietly become a multi-sprint engineering effort nobody planned for.

Meanwhile, sixty easier findings are sitting beside it.

Configuration flags.

Minor version bumps.

Small dependency updates.

Things that can actually fit inside a sprint.

So teams do something entirely rational.

They close the easy findings.

The dashboard gets greener.

The closure rate improves.

And five difficult, architecturally entangled exposures move into the next quarter.

Then the next.

There is an uncomfortable possibility here:

The vulnerability program can appear to be improving while scarce engineering capacity is being spent on the wrong risk.

That is not primarily a patching problem.

It is an allocation problem.

And I think product companies need to start treating it as one.

The scanner counts vulnerabilities. The CTO spends engineering capacity.

Those are different currencies.

Industry data increasingly shows why the distinction matters.

Veracode's 2026 State of Software Security report analyzed approximately 1.6 million applications and reported that 82% of organizations carry security debt.

Critical security debt increased 20% year over year.

And roughly 66% of the most dangerous, longest-lived vulnerabilities were associated with open-source or third-party dependencies. [1]

That last number should get the attention of any product engineering leader.

Because a third-party vulnerability may appear in the security platform as one finding.

To Engineering, it can mean:

upgrade the framework,

change application code,

rebuild a shared base image,

test downstream integrations,

coordinate with another team,

and potentially change an interface a customer depends on.

A vulnerability ticket can hide a quarter of engineering work.

The Verizon 2026 DBIR vulnerability-remediation analysis contributed by Qualys tells the capacity story from another angle.

Across more than one billion KEV-linked records, KEV-associated vulnerability instances increased approximately 7.7x over four years.

But median detection-to-closure remained around nine days.

Meanwhile, the number of still-open instances at day 28 expanded from roughly 31 million to 184 million. [2]

That distinction matters.

The engine did not necessarily get slower.

The volume outran fixed capacity.

That requires a different management response.

You cannot solve a capacity problem by generating more tickets.

AI may make the gap wider, not smaller

There is another force entering this equation.

AI.

ProjectDiscovery's 2026 AI Coding Impact Report, based on a survey of 200 practitioners, reported that all respondents had seen increased engineering delivery over the previous year, with 49% attributing most of that acceleration to AI-assisted coding.

At the same time, 62% of security teams said keeping pace was becoming harder, and the report described security teams spending substantial time validating findings rather than fixing them. [3]

This is industry survey evidence, not a universal law.

But the direction is important.

AI is increasing our ability to:

write software,

change software,

discover weaknesses,

produce findings,

and eventually recommend fixes.

What AI does not automatically manufacture is:

more regression-testing capacity,

more change windows,

more architecture attention,

more product capacity,

or unlimited engineers.

So I think we are approaching an important inversion.

For years, the vulnerability-management problem was:

“How do we find more?”

Increasingly, the harder question may be:

“What deserves the next engineering week?”

That is where Remediation Economics enters the conversation.

Remediation Economics is not another score

I do not want to create another number to compete with CVSS, EPSS, KEV, or SCOPE.

The problem is not that we lack scores.

The missing decision comes later.

Earlier in this series, SCOPE helped answer:

Which exposures actually matter?

The Intervention Ladder helped answer:

What treatment options do we have?

Remediation Economics addresses the next question:

Which intervention deserves scarce engineering capacity?

My definition is:

Remediation Economics is choosing the intervention that produces the greatest defensible reduction in consequential exposure for the engineering effort, operational disruption, and time required.

I think of it as a ratio lens, not a universal mathematical formula.

On one side:

meaningful exposure reduction.

On the other:

engineering effort, disruption, time, and durability.

The objective is not to manufacture a precise score such as 8.73.

It is to make the trade-off visible.

Because the most severe vulnerability is not automatically the best use of the next engineering week.

And the easiest vulnerability to close certainly is not.

A real worked example shows why the treatment matters

A NIST-hosted IoT vulnerability-management case study presented by Asimily gives us a useful concrete example.

The worked case considered 91 vulnerability-related problems.

Treating each one with the same full remediation approach, estimated at approximately fourteen engineering hours per finding, would require:

1,274 engineering hours.

Once the findings were prioritized by risk and matched to more appropriately scoped interventions, the estimated effort for a comparable risk-management objective fell to approximately:

127 hours. [4]

That is roughly a 90% reduction in estimated effort in that particular worked example.

I would not turn that into a claim that every organization can reduce remediation effort by 90%.

It cannot.

It is one case study.

But it proves a much more useful point:

The remediation strategy changes the economics.

The question is not merely whether a vulnerability gets treated.

It is whether we chose the right treatment for the risk we were trying to remove.

Here is the part vulnerability dashboards usually miss: opportunity cost

Suppose Engineering has ten weeks of capacity available for material security work.

You can spend those ten weeks upgrading one deeply embedded framework.

Or removing an exposed management path.

Or replacing an EOL component.

Or fixing thirty independent vulnerabilities.

Whichever choice you make, the other options do not disappear.

That gives remediation an opportunity cost.

The real cost of remediating Vulnerability A is not only the engineering effort spent on A. It is also the risk reduction we chose not to buy somewhere else with those same engineering hours.

Security programs rarely make that visible.

Investment committees do it all the time.

If we have $10 million, which programs receive it?

If we have twenty engineers, which products receive them?

If we have one quarter, which transformation gets funded?

But the moment the discussion becomes vulnerability remediation, we often revert to:

Critical first.
Oldest first.
SLA breach first.
Whatever escalated loudest first.

That is not portfolio allocation.

That is queue management.

A vulnerability queue tells you what is waiting. It does not tell you what your next unit of engineering capacity should buy.

Sometimes the best remediation does not patch the vulnerability

Article 3 introduced the idea of breaking the attack path.

Here is why that matters economically.

Imagine a vulnerable internal management service.

Patching it requires a major platform upgrade, regression testing, and a difficult maintenance window.

But the service does not actually need to be reachable from the network path an attacker would use.

Removing that route may eliminate the exploitable condition far faster than upgrading the application.

The CVE still exists.

The usable path does not.

That does not mean “never patch.”

It means:

Patch is an intervention. Risk reduction is the outcome.

One architecture change at the right choke point can sometimes eliminate multiple attack opportunities for less engineering effort than repairing every component individually.

This is where “100 patches versus one architecture change” stops being rhetoric.

It becomes an investment comparison.

Engineering capacity is a security control

This may be the most important reframe in the article.

We normally describe security controls as things like:

MFA,

segmentation,

EDR,

encryption,

monitoring,

patching,

and access controls.

But in a product company, another control determines whether known exposure actually gets removed:

available engineering capacity.

Security cannot modernize your authentication stack without engineering.

Security cannot retire an EOL platform.

It cannot rewrite a shared dependency.

It cannot redesign a brittle API.

It cannot safely replace a component touching twenty production services.

Security can identify the exposure.

Explain it.

Prioritize it.

Challenge assumptions.

Verify the result.

But somebody still has to change the product.

So:

A security SLA cannot manufacture engineering capacity.

If leadership consistently allocates 100% of Engineering to features, reliability, and customer commitments and then asks Security why structural vulnerabilities remain open, the operating model is contradicting itself.

You cannot claim to prioritize security while refusing to prioritize the engineering capacity required to change the system.

I would treat remediation capacity like capital

Product companies already understand portfolio allocation.

So I would apply the same discipline to consequential exposure.

I propose a simple executive operating model:

RESERVE → COMPARE → PROMOTE → VERIFY

Not another security framework for the wall.

Four leadership decisions.

1. RESERVE

Decide before the quarter starts how much engineering capacity will be protected for material exposure reduction.

I am deliberately not prescribing 10%, 15%, or 20%.

There is no universal right number.

But there should be a number.

Or an explicit capacity commitment.

And it should be agreed by Engineering, Security, and Product before every vulnerability has to fight individually for roadmap space.

Because:

Security capacity that disappears every time feature pressure rises was never protected capacity.

2. COMPARE

For the material exposures that matter, compare the intervention options.

Ask:

How much consequential exposure disappears?

How much engineering capacity does this consume?

What operational disruption could it create?

How long until the exposure actually falls?

Is the treatment durable?

This is where patch, upgrade, mitigate, break-the-path, isolate, replace, retire, and temporary acceptance can be evaluated against the same outcome.

Not:

Which ticket is easiest to close?

But:

Which intervention buys the strongest defensible reduction in exposure?

3. PROMOTE

Some remediation work does not belong in a vulnerability backlog.

This matters enormously.

If fixing an exposure requires:

three engineering teams,

a major identity migration,

platform replacement,

customer transition,

multi-quarter funding,

or architecture redesign,

stop pretending it is simply an overdue vulnerability.

Promote it.

Move it into the technology-investment or architecture portfolio.

Give it:

an executive sponsor,

funding,

a roadmap,

an exit outcome,

and visible residual risk while the transformation occurs.

An EOL platform replacement is not a 30-day patch ticket that happens to be late for eighteen months.

It is an unfunded modernization decision masquerading as a vulnerability.

That distinction changes the conversation completely.

4. VERIFY

At the end of the quarter, do not ask only:

“How many findings did we close?”

Ask:

“What consequential exposure did the engineering capacity we spent actually remove?”

Did attacker reachability disappear?

Was a privileged path eliminated?

Was an unsupported platform retired?

Did the vulnerable dependency actually leave production?

Did an architectural intervention eliminate recurrence?

Did the compensating control perform as expected?

Engineering effort is the input. Risk reduction is the return.

Three executive rules I would put into governance

If I were tightening the operating model tomorrow, I would add three rules.

Rule 1 — If it cannot fit in the vulnerability backlog, promote it.

If a remediation needs a quarter, multiple teams, or meaningful capital, stop measuring it as though somebody forgot to update a ticket.

Make the investment decision explicit.

Rule 2 — Repeated roadmap displacement becomes explicit risk acceptance.

If a high-value remediation loses its capacity to feature work once, that may be normal portfolio pressure.

If it happens again, I would want the next deferral made by someone with authority to accept the consequence.

Because:

If protected security work is repeatedly displaced by roadmap work, the organization is accepting cyber risk through scheduling — whether anyone signs a risk-acceptance form or not.

That deserves visibility.

Rule 3 — Compare architectural treatment before buying the expensive patch.

If segmentation, privilege reduction, route removal, isolation, retirement, or another architecture change can eliminate the same meaningful attacker opportunity faster and more durably, put that option beside the patch before committing capacity.

Not because architecture is always cheaper.

It is not.

Because it deserves to be compared.

Two metrics I would put in front of executives

There are two measures I think can change the conversation.

Neither is an industry standard.

I am proposing them as management lenses.

Security Capacity Yield

Ask:

How much consequential exposure did the engineering capacity reserved for security actually reduce?

I would not pretend that every company can turn that into one perfectly comparable number.

Initially, trend it.

Compare quarters.

Compare intervention categories.

Compare teams.

Did twenty engineering weeks this quarter remove more consequential exposure than twenty weeks last quarter?

If not, why?

That is a far more interesting leadership discussion than “we closed 14% more findings.”

Deferred High-Value Exposure

Track material exposure where:

a credible high-value intervention exists,

the organization understands it,

but capacity or funding was deliberately allocated somewhere else.

That metric tells leadership:

what risk reduction it chose not to buy.

Imagine the executive review saying:

Eleven material exposures remain.
Seven have active treatment.
Two are temporarily mitigated.
Two high-value interventions were deferred because their engineering capacity was reassigned to roadmap commitments.

That is an honest risk conversation.

Those two findings are no longer “Security backlog.”

They are leadership allocation decisions.

The product-company strategy is different from the compliance strategy

This distinction matters.

In product companies — including many EdTech environments

EdTech is not “unregulated.”

Student privacy, FERPA, COPPA, GDPR, state requirements, contracts, and other obligations can all matter depending on product and geography.

But the forcing function for infrastructure modernization may still be less automatic than in environments where security-control requirements are highly prescriptive.

That means security capacity often has to be deliberately manufactured.

My strategy would be:

Protect capacity before quarterly planning closes. Do not ask for whatever is left after the roadmap is full.

Connect exposure reduction to product health. Resilience, enterprise readiness, customer trust, reliability, incident reduction, and supportability are Product concerns too.

Use modernization windows aggressively. If Engineering is already changing authentication, containers, APIs, or a platform, that may be the cheapest moment to retire related Exposure Debt.

Bring remediation decisions into portfolio planning. Do not keep consequential security work trapped inside a Security-only meeting Product never attends.

The essential point is:

security work has to compete transparently for engineering capacity, not arrive as an unexplained tax after capacity has already been committed.

In regulated industries, the danger can be different

Finance, healthcare, payments, and similar environments often have stronger forcing functions.

Audit findings get funded.

Regulatory commitments get deadlines.

Control gaps receive executive attention.

That is valuable.

But it creates another danger:

compliance can accidentally become the engineering-allocation algorithm.

The organization spends capacity on what must be evidenced for the audit while the architectural intervention that would eliminate more consequential exposure remains unfunded because nobody explicitly required it.

So I would ask two questions separately:

What are we required to fix?

and:

What treatment creates the greatest meaningful exposure reduction?

Sometimes they produce the same answer.

Sometimes they do not.

Compliance is an important floor.

It should not automatically become the entire remediation strategy.

What should Engineering actually be rewarded for?

This is where I think product companies need to be careful.

If Engineering is rewarded for:

tickets closed,

sprint throughput,

release velocity,

and SLA compliance,

then it should not surprise us when teams optimize for work that improves those numbers.

Engineering is not irrational.

The incentives are.

I would rather see secure engineering treated as a product-quality outcome.

Not:

“Engineering completed Security's vulnerabilities.”

But:

“Engineering produced a more secure and resilient product.”

That means recognizing:

durable architectural reduction,

retirement of recurring exposure,

attack-path elimination,

dependency modernization,

and measurable reduction in attackability.

Engineering teams should not be rewarded for producing vulnerability closure. They should be rewarded for producing secure, resilient products — and measurable exposure reduction is part of that engineering outcome.

The Monday-morning exercise

Take the twenty most important items in your current remediation backlog.

Not the twenty highest CVSS scores automatically.

The twenty exposures you genuinely believe deserve attention.

For each, write down:

Business consequence

Current exploitability or threat

Relevant attack path

Engineering effort

Operational disruption

Alternative interventions

Time to meaningful reduction

Durability

Then give the leadership team a constraint:

You have ten engineering weeks.

Where do you spend them?

Do not allow the answer:

“All twenty are important.”

Of course they are.

That is not the exercise.

The exercise is allocation.

Now compare the result with what Engineering is actually working on.

Where the lists diverge, ask why.

Maybe you will uncover a genuine customer dependency.

A testing constraint.

A change-window problem.

An architecture dependency.

Those are useful discoveries.

But you may find something more uncomfortable.

Engineering capacity may be going toward the work that makes the vulnerability dashboard improve fastest rather than the work that reduces consequential exposure fastest.

That is worth knowing.

My position

I do not think the engineering goal should be:

Fix more vulnerabilities.

The goal should be:

Remove more consequential exposure.

Sometimes that means patching.

Sometimes upgrading.

Sometimes changing privilege.

Sometimes breaking the attack path.

Sometimes isolating.

Sometimes retiring a platform.

And sometimes leadership may consciously carry residual exposure while funding the durable solution.

But the decision should expose the trade-off:

What risk reduction are we buying?

How much engineering capacity does it consume?

What could it disrupt?

How quickly does it reduce exposure?

How durable is the result?

And what risk reduction are we giving up by spending that capacity here rather than somewhere else?

Because:

Engineering capacity is finite. Security findings increasingly are not.

Once you accept that, the vulnerability backlog stops looking like a queue.

It starts looking like an investment portfolio.

And that leads to the argument I would put in front of any CISO, CTO, Product leader, or engineering executive:

A remediation backlog is a capital-allocation problem disguised as a security problem.

Protect the capacity.

Compare the interventions.

Promote structural work into the investment portfolio.

Verify what exposure actually disappeared.

And stop asking Engineering how many vulnerabilities it closed when the real question was always:

Did leadership spend scarce engineering capacity on the risk reduction that mattered most?


Next in the series

When AI Finds Exposure Faster Than Organizations Can Retire It

This series began with a simple observation:

we are getting dramatically better at finding vulnerabilities.

Now the other side of the equation is becoming clearer.

AI is accelerating software creation.

Discovery velocity is increasing.

Organizations are carrying accumulated Exposure Debt.

Engineering capacity remains finite.

The next question is what happens when:

discovery velocity exceeds organizational risk-reduction capacity.

Because the next bottleneck may not be finding exposure.

It may be deciding what deserves action — and having the organizational capacity to finish the decision.
